\chapter{Experimental Evaluation}\label{ch:m-results}

\section{Evaluation Strategy}

Results follow the decision process. Point-to-point and structured microbenchmarks identify message-size and topology regions in which each stack is competitive. The \texttt{cg\_step} benchmark tests a CG-like composition, and the complete aCG campaign determines whether that ranking survives irregular sparse matrices, repeated dependencies, overlap, and convergence. A cross-level summary closes the main campaign; the subsequent preliminary NCCL section reports the later memory-path comparison separately.

Small differences must be interpreted against job-level spread. The discussion therefore emphasizes persistent regions, large crossovers, and application-level effects rather than declaring a winner from isolated one-percent differences.

\section{Experimental Environment}\label{sec:eval-environment}

Every measurement reported in this chapter was collected on the accelerated Booster partition of the Leonardo supercomputer at CINECA \cite{turisini_leonardo_2024}, whose architecture Section~\ref{sec:leonardo} describes: four NVIDIA A100 SXM GPUs per node, joined inside the node by NVLink~3.0 without an NVSwitch and between nodes by an InfiniBand HDR DragonFly+ fabric reached through two dual-port HDR100 adapters. One process drives one GPU throughout, so a topology label names both the node count and the ranks per node, from the single-GPU control \texttt{1n1g} to 32 GPUs on eight nodes in \texttt{8n4g}. Jobs are scheduled through Slurm, and each benchmark cell is measured in several independently scheduled allocations so that job-to-job placement variation is visible in the reported spread rather than hidden inside one lucky allocation.

The evidence accumulated in this way is substantial enough to be worth stating before the results begin. The microbenchmark campaign sweeps four communication patterns across the topology matrix and the full message-size range of each stack below; the composite \texttt{cg\_step} sweep covers 210 topology, grid-size, and backend cells with nine trials each; and the aCG campaign contributes 507 completed solver logs, of which 354 are strict four-communicator trials. Section~\ref{sec:benchmark-method} defines the patterns and the timing and validation policy that every cell obeys, and Section~\ref{sec:acg-method} the two SuiteSparse matrices that the solver campaign uses.

Two properties of this environment govern how the comparisons may be read. MPI and OSHMPI resolve one HPC-X installation and share a single UCX configuration, so a difference between them is attributable to the interface and its operation mapping rather than to the transport underneath. NCCL and NVSHMEM instead select their own payload transports, so a difference involving them is a difference between complete stacks. The runtime policies that complete this picture -- per-operation UCC selection, the \texttt{ibrc} remote transport, symmetric heap sizes, and process-to-GPU mapping -- belong to the experimental design and are recorded in Section~\ref{sec:runtime-method} rather than repeated here. Where the aCG campaign deliberately departs from the benchmark configuration, in its UCX rendezvous threshold and its disabling of NCCL dispatch beneath NVSHMEM, the size of each deviation was measured rather than assumed, and Section~\ref{sec:acg-results} reports both.

The SYCL aCG comparison of Section~\ref{sec:acg-results} uses the same DPC++ toolchain as the benchmark's SYCL stacks, with MPI providing its distributed runtime and a solver option selecting MPI or oneCCL for the scalar reductions. Its reporting policy differs from the CUDA campaign's, which is why its results stay separate rather than joining the primary ranking.

One consequence of this environment deserves stating plainly, because it bounds the whole chapter. The NCCL and NVSHMEM versions are those the installed NVHPC~24.5 module provides, not versions selected for the study, and NVSHMEM~2.11.0 predates the release series in which several device-initiated paths matured. The preliminary comparison in Section~\ref{sec:nccl-preliminary-results} steps outside this environment on purpose: it adds a separate NCCL~2.31.2 build on a single four-GPU node to ask whether a newer library and symmetric memory registration would change the small-message picture. It is reported apart from the main campaign because its toolchain, and much of its measurement metadata, are not those recorded above.

\subsection{Halo Exchange}

For isolated halo exchanges, MPI has the lowest fixed overhead on every measured topology. Its fitted small-message floor ranges from approximately 4.8\,$\mu$s on \texttt{1n2g} to 6.6\,$\mu$s on \texttt{8n4g}; NCCL, oneCCL, and the SHMEM paths pay higher launch or synchronization costs. At large multi-node messages, NVSHMEM overtakes MPI. On \texttt{8n4g} the two are effectively tied at a 2\,MiB aggregate payload, while at 4\,MiB NVSHMEM requires 155.860\,$\mu$s and CUDA MPI 166.488\,$\mu$s.

Steady-state batching changes the result because fixed completion costs are amortized. OSHMPI becomes competitive at small-to-medium sizes, while NVSHMEM gives the highest large-message rate. \subsection{Allreduce}

Allreduce separates the small-message synchronization regime from the bulk-reduction regime. UCC improves the GPU-aware MPI result by 1.1--140 times across all sizes on \texttt{1n2g}, \texttt{1n4g}, and \texttt{2n1g}, and it helps at and above 64\,KiB on the larger multi-node topologies. Below that threshold, it is 1.06--2.2 times slower. Every topology with four GPUs per node also exhibits a reproducible cliff at exactly 4\,KiB: UCC latency rises by 81\% on \texttt{1n4g} and 120\% on \texttt{8n4g} between 2 and 4\,KiB while the non-UCC path remains flat. This boundary is not inferred from timing alone. TL/UCP's default rule explicitly selects latency-oriented recursive k-nomial below 4\,KiB and bandwidth-oriented scatter-reduce/allgather k-nomial at and above 4\,KiB \cite{openucx_ucc_source_2026}. The source-level switch explains why both CUDA and SYCL MPI reproduce the cliff. The selected policy nevertheless enables UCC for this benchmark because it is decisively better over most of the payload range.

Figure~\ref{fig:m-ucc-allreduce} shows the available matched A/B surface behind this policy. Plotting the ratio rather than either absolute curve makes both the topology-dependent small-message penalty and the large-message gain visible on one scale.

\subsection{All-to-All}

The all-to-all results demonstrate why runtime tuning must precede library ranking. Without UCC, Open MPI takes 338--422\,$\mu$s on \texttt{4n4g} and 670--974\,$\mu$s on \texttt{8n4g} for transfers no larger than 256 bytes per peer. Enabling UCC reduces these cells to 14--31\,$\mu$s, a 17--34 times improvement. The apparent small-message advantage of NCCL and NVSHMEM therefore disappears: tuned MPI is 2.7--5 times faster in this region. UCC's large-message regression is real but bounded at 1.8 times and appears above approximately 16\,KiB per peer on multi-node topologies, making the default a trade of at most 1.8 times against as much as 34 times.

Figure~\ref{fig:m-ucc-alltoall} localizes that trade to communicator and payload size. The sharp high-rank region below the marked threshold motivates enabling UCC by default; the smaller bars below parity at large payloads show the cost of that choice.

Modified Bruck reduces the number of network rounds to $\lceil\log_2 P\rceil$ by forwarding blocks, a useful latency trade on the host systems for which the algorithm was designed \cite{bruck_alltoall_1997}. Open MPI's implementation, however, allocates a temporary span for the complete send buffer, performs initial and final cyclic rotations, constructs and commits an indexed datatype at every distance, and copies each received noncontiguous block set back before the next round \cite{openmpi_alltoall_source_2026}. With GPU-resident data, these datatype-engine copies and noncontiguous datatype operations are no longer cache-resident host work. The plateau is consequently independent of payload from 4 to 256 bytes per peer, and doubling from 16 to 32 ranks changes its cost by 1.97--1.99 times rather than the $5/4$ ratio expected if Bruck's network rounds alone dominated. This scaling is consistent with local buffer and datatype work dominating, although it is not a direct profile of each copy.

UCC avoids creating that cost. The runtime trace maps every measured CUDA all-to-all size to \texttt{CL\_BASIC \{TL\_UCP\}}, and TL/UCP source identifies its CUDA default as the pairwise two-sided implementation \cite{venkata_ucc_2024,openucx_ucc_source_2026}. That implementation addresses one contiguous slice per peer in the original CUDA send and receive buffers and calls UCX's nonblocking tagged send and receive functions with the CUDA memory type. It has no UCC-layer full-buffer rotation, indexed datatype, or Bruck temporary span. UCC is still faster than Open MPI's forced linear schedules, but by the ordinary factor of approximately 1.5 rather than 17--34 times. The large ratio therefore measures the cost of Open MPI's unsuitable default; forcing \texttt{linear\_sync} is another way to avoid it without UCC.

As the per-peer payload grows, NCCL and NVSHMEM overtake MPI. The sampled crossover moves to smaller messages as the rank count increases: between 32 and 64\,KiB per peer on \texttt{2n4g}, between 8 and 16\,KiB on \texttt{4n4g}, and between 2 and 4\,KiB on \texttt{8n4g}. Each retained cell still rests on three independently scheduled allocations and nine trials, and an effect of 17--34 times is far too large to arise from allocation variation, but a fully matched comparison at the allocation level would require one clean final sweep.

\subsection{OSHMPI Scalar-Reduction Placement}

The winning OSHMPI row requires a qualification because its bulk data and scalar reductions use different memory paths. In staged mode, ordinary CUDA allocations hold the dot-product partials; each reduction copies one double to the default host symmetric heap and executes \texttt{shmem\_double\_sum\_to\_all} there. In device mode, the partials, result, and OpenSHMEM work arrays instead reside in the OSHMPI CUDA symmetric space, and UCC is enabled so that the collective can consume device pointers. A paired experiment at side~512 measured nine trials per cell against a staged baseline whose job-to-job spread was 1.00--1.03 times. Table~\ref{tab:m-oshmpi-reduce-placement} reports the relative increase caused by the complete device/UCC configuration.

The reduction result is the expected part of the experiment. Direct device reduction costs 1.88--2.45 times the staged reduction phase because, for an eight-byte collective, UCC's small-message overhead exceeds the cost of copying the two scalar operands to the host. Host staging is therefore a measured optimization for this specific payload, not merely a compatibility workaround. It is not a general recommendation to stage reductions: in the standalone 16\,MiB allreduce on \texttt{1n4g}, staging is 34 times slower than the device path because pageable PCIe traffic dominates the bulk operation.

The 47--70\% unsplit-step penalty must not be attributed entirely to the collective. The compute phase performs no communication but rises by 45--47\%, nearly independently of topology. The device variant changes operand residency, enables UCC, and replaces ordinary CUDA partial buffers with allocations from the OSHMPI CUDA symmetric space. Its flat compute penalty is consistent with slower kernel writes to those registered symmetric allocations, but the present experiment does not isolate that mechanism. A retained \texttt{1n1g} device-versus-staged control, where no inter-rank collective or RMA occurs, is required to distinguish the allocator effect from UCC or communication effects.

\subsection{Primitive-to-Step Model}

The CUDA MPI phase measurements permit a direct test of the isolated primitives. Once the allreduce microbenchmark is matched to the UCC setting used by \texttt{cg\_step}, it predicts the measured CUDA MPI reduction phase to within 8\% at \texttt{1n4g} and 6\% at \texttt{4n4g}. Without that correction, the prediction is 31\% high at one topology and 12\% low at the other because the sign of UCC's effect changes with placement. The payload comparison is valid despite different element types: the microbenchmark reduces two \texttt{float32} values and \texttt{cg\_step} reduces one \texttt{float64}, for eight bytes in both cases. The default OSHMPI allreduce curve cannot be substituted into the same model because it measures device/UCC operands, whereas the winning OSHMPI \texttt{cg\_step} row stages its scalar operands through host memory.

The halo prediction fails by a stable structural factor. \texttt{halo\_1d} predicts 11.6--12.2\,$\mu$s while the CUDA MPI step measures 22--24\,$\mu$s; the ratio is 1.88 at \texttt{1n4g} and 1.90 at \texttt{4n4g} and remains flat across an eightfold payload change. The discrepancy follows from operation count rather than bandwidth: \texttt{cg\_step} issues two serialized \texttt{MPI\_Sendrecv} calls, whereas \texttt{halo\_1d} executes one ring exchange.

\subsection{Residual SYCL Cost}

The CUDA and SYCL MPI microbenchmarks agree within approximately 1--2\% on the same HPC-X, UCX, and UCC stack, but their composite steps do not. Five redundant waits between submissions on the in-order SYCL queue accounted for 22.7\,$\mu$s in SYCL MPI and 24.5\,$\mu$s in oneCCL; removing them recovered only about one third of the original gap. Measurements taken before that fix describe a different implementation and are therefore excluded from the final sweep rather than pooled with it.

The final single-GPU control isolates what remains. At side~512, SYCL MPI takes 116.9\,$\mu$s against CUDA MPI's 79.3\,$\mu$s, a fixed gap of 37.6\,$\mu$s without communication. At side~8192 the values are 1159.8 and 956.5\,$\mu$s, indicating approximately 19\% slower arithmetic in addition to the roughly 38\,$\mu$s submission cost. The use of \texttt{sycl::reduction} instead of the hand-written CUDA dot kernel is the next hypothesis to test, not an established cause.

Taken alone, these results predict OSHMPI throughout the multi-node CG-like regime. The complete solver and its irregular communication pattern provide a direct test of that prediction.

\subsection{Overlap and Synchronization}

The growing OSHMPI penalty is consistent with its communication path. For Bump, time attributed to the residual ``other'' category grows from approximately 1.04\,s at two GPUs to 3.71\,s at 32; Queen grows from approximately 1.10\,s to 3.69\,s. The nominal halo column remains near 0.06--0.08\,s because it brackets the end function, which performs no communication for OSHMPI. The blocking put/quiet/barrier sequence has already completed in the begin function and is charged to ``other''.

Two exploratory changes did not improve the total. Replacing global barriers with neighbor flags left the representative result essentially unchanged, and splitting begin/end still called \texttt{shmem\_quiet} before computation, merely moving measured time between categories. These negative results indicate that the barrier alone is not the dominant issue. Genuine overlap requires nonblocking puts in the begin phase and a deferred quiet in the end phase, followed by instrumentation that separates issue time from network wait time.

This blocking path contributes to the disagreement with \texttt{cg\_step}, but it is not the only cause. The synthetic comparison uses two fixed neighbors, eager messages, blocking exclusively timed exchange, and a 4\,KiB per-peer message at side~512. Across aCG, ranks instead communicate with 1.0--10.8 irregular neighbors and exchange 125--336\,KiB per iteration through nonblocking rendezvous messages overlapped with the local sparse matrix--vector product. Its reported halo time is only the exposed wait: the implied 48--108\,GB/s per process can exceed any one NIC and confirms that it is not measuring the same quantity as the synthetic halo. The different UCX threshold is not the explanation; the controlled threshold change affects \texttt{cg\_step} by only 1.8--3.0\%. Peer structure, protocol, and overlap place the solver in a regime that neither \texttt{cg\_step} nor the regular \texttt{halo\_1d} ring contains.

\subsection{Convergence and Reliability}

All 90 completed multi-GPU OSHMPI trials terminate successfully and satisfy the relative residual threshold. Bump residuals range from $9.132\times10^{-7}$ to $9.998\times10^{-7}$, and Queen residuals from $9.044\times10^{-7}$ to $9.998\times10^{-7}$. Manufactured-solution error remains tightly grouped across backends. This supports numerical consistency of the communicator port.

Reduction order changes the iteration count by a few percent and occasionally produces outliers. One Bump two-GPU OSHMPI run required 30,611 iterations and caused a $1.23\times$ spread; repeat jobs returned to the approximately 25,000-iteration region. For this reason throughput normalized by executed work is more robust than total time alone.

MPI exhibits a separate reliability problem at larger scales, localized to the many-neighbor communication path rather than to \texttt{MPI\_Allreduce} itself. The \texttt{acg-cg-mpi} and \texttt{acg-cg-oshmpi} variants call the identical CUDA-aware collective on the same stack and differ only in whether the halo uses MPI nonblocking messages or OSHMPI puts. On Bump at \texttt{4n4g}, the former allreduce has a 176.8\,$\mu$s median and 29.6-fold spread, while the latter is 29.0\,$\mu$s with a 1.0-fold spread; at \texttt{8n4g}, the medians are 1103.8 and 30.5\,$\mu$s. Halo and allreduce inflate together run by run, showing that the MPI halo leaves state or arrival skew that poisons the following collective measurement.

The instability is not allocation-bound. Within one allocation, spread reaches 29.6 times for Bump and 34.5 times for Queen at \texttt{4n4g}; one \texttt{8n4g} job records three sequential allreduce measurements of 157, 2051, and 152\,$\mu$s on the same nodes. NCCL remains stable on those allocations, arguing against node selection or general fabric congestion as the cause. The evidence supports distinct fast and slow Open MPI/UCX states on the many-neighbor rendezvous path, but not a formal claim of bimodality. One 32-GPU attempt additionally reports PMIx/UCX failure, and some timing decompositions become self-inconsistent even when the final solution is valid. \texttt{cg\_step} does not reproduce this behavior, so NCCL remains the stable performance reference and MPI is reported through per-job ranges rather than a pooled median or rare fastest point.

\section{Cross-Level Summary}

The three evidence levels agree on broad mechanisms but not on a total backend order. Once paired by the actual collective call and runtime settings, \texttt{cg\_step} predicts aCG's eight-byte reduction within 6.5--9.7\% for MPI and NCCL and within 14--21\% for NVSHMEM; the independent scaling factors agree to within 1--5\%. The stable 1.9-fold difference between \texttt{halo\_1d} and the \texttt{cg\_step} halo is explained by two serialized exchanges, but neither synthetic halo predicts aCG's irregular, rendezvous, mostly overlapped exchange. The complete solver ranking consequently cannot be obtained by adding isolated costs: \texttt{cg\_step}'s OSHMPI leadership does not survive aCG's peer structure, overlap opportunities, collective configuration, convergence, and MPI performance regimes.

\begin{resultbox}[Main experimental result]
Pattern-oriented benchmarks predict matched primitives and identify the mechanisms that matter, but they predict a complete application only when peer structure, payload, synchronization, progress, and overlap semantics also match. On the evaluated aCG campaign, NCCL is the fastest stable host-initiated backend even though OSHMPI wins every multi-rank synthetic CG-step cell.
\end{resultbox}

\subsection{All-to-All: Limited Small-Message Benefit}

For the supplied 64\,B--32\,KiB cells, the symmetric and plain NCCL~2.31.2 medians are nearly equal, and both remain slower than NCCL~2.18.5. At 16\,MiB, the symmetric median is 89.2\,$\mu$s versus 106.1\,$\mu$s for plain 2.31.2 and 102.2\,$\mu$s for 2.18.5: approximately $1.19\times$ and $1.15\times$ speedups, respectively. Thus the small-message allreduce advantage does not generalize to personalized exchange.

The note attributes this contrast to the absence of a dedicated all-to-all kernel in NCCL's internal symmetric-kernel directory. That observation alone cannot establish which path the executable selected or why it was slower. A host all-to-all call, grouped sends/receives, and a custom LSA device kernel are different implementations. Version-specific API coverage and registration benefits must therefore be checked against the tested executable before assigning a causal explanation \cite{nvidia_nccl_collectives_2026,nvidia_nccl_registration_2026}. In particular, this study supports neither a universal rejection of LSA all-to-all nor a new aCG prediction, since CG contains no all-to-all phase.

\subsection{Inter-Node Observations and Application Implications}

The same note reports that the tested GIN CPU-proxy path did not improve on the older NCCL reference across nodes, and that attempted GDAKI runs stalled. It supplies no inter-node timing table or diagnostic logs, so no numerical comparison or definitive failure cause is inferred. The independently documented NVSHMEM IBGDA driver limitations provide platform context, not proof that the NCCL failure had the same cause. LSA's intra-node peer-memory path remains distinct from GDAKI's network-posting path.

The strongest candidate for follow-up is therefore the small allreduce on one node. A matched one-double FP64 sum, followed by a reduction-only change to \texttt{cg\_step} and aCG, would test whether the observed latency gain survives application dependencies. A hybrid local-LSA/inter-node-MPI reduction would add local reduction, inter-node reduction, and local distribution stages; its extra synchronization could outweigh the gain for eight-byte operands. It remains a hypothesis rather than a demonstrated improvement.
