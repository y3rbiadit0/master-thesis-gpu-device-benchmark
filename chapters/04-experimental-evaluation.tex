\chapter{Experimental Results}\label{ch:m-results}

This chapter follows the evaluation levels of Section~\ref{sec:research-planning-levels}: first the communication microbenchmarks, then the \texttt{moe} and \texttt{cg\_step} application microbenchmarks, and finally the aCG solver. Across all of them, the performance of a backend depends strongly on the communication pattern and on the configuration of the underlying libraries.

Each section follows the same topology progression, from \texttt{1n2g} and \texttt{1n4g} on NVLink, through \texttt{2n1g} on a single InfiniBand link, to \texttt{2n4g}--\texttt{8n4g} with both domains active. The question it answers throughout is whether a stack that performs well within a node keeps that advantage once communication crosses the network.

Benchmark curves show the median of allocation means with their range, as defined in Section~\ref{sec:execution-procedure}; the main figures use representative topologies, and the complete panels are in Appendix~\ref{app:complete-results}. Results available only as summaries are identified where they appear, and the preliminary NCCL study is kept in Appendix~\ref{app:nccl-preliminary}.

\section{Communication Microbenchmarks}\label{sec:micro-results}

Each pattern is presented with the complete set of measured backends for that campaign, followed immediately by the configuration and execution-contract evidence needed to interpret the curves.

\subsection{Ping-Pong}

The first comparison asks whether device-side invocation improves a dependency-ordered exchange. Figure~\ref{fig:m-pingpong-regimes} shows all six measured paths. Across nodes, CUDA MPI has the lowest latency throughout the sweep. At four bytes its allocation-based median is 3.36\,$\mu$s, compared with 7.28\,$\mu$s for OSHMPI, 17.17\,$\mu$s for NVSHMEM, and 28.78\,$\mu$s for NCCL. Device invocation therefore does not remove the startup advantage of the evaluated MPI path.

\label{fig:m-pingpong-regimes}
\end{figure}

Within a node, NVSHMEM becomes competitive over an intermediate message range, while MPI again improves relative to it at large payloads. Table~\ref{tab:m-pingpong-inter} also shows that CUDA and SYCL MPI cannot be assumed to agree at every size: their small-message results are close to the MPI startup regime, but their large-message endpoints differ substantially. A shared MPI installation supplies a useful comparison, not a guarantee of identical execution costs.

The common-interface NCCL path is also visible in this startup-sensitive pattern. At four-byte inter-node ping-pong, oneCCL/NCCL takes 33.8\,$\mu$s against 28.8\,$\mu$s for direct NCCL, approximately 17\% more. This changes the compute interface and orchestration as well as the adapter call, so it measures the complete oneCCL/NCCL stack rather than isolated wrapper overhead. The ping-pong result is consequently a small-message half-round-trip comparison, not an estimate of an initiation-only launch saving.

\begin{keypoint}[Ping-pong takeaway]
MPI owns this pattern: lowest latency at every size across nodes, and the reference for startup cost. OSHMPI is the closest alternative at small messages, at roughly twice MPI's four-byte latency. NVSHMEM is competitive only over an intermediate intra-node range, and its device-invoked variant does not recover the startup gap. NCCL is an order of magnitude behind at four bytes, which is the cost of its stream-completion contract rather than of its transport. oneCCL/NCCL adds about 17\% to direct NCCL, and SYCL MPI tracks CUDA MPI at small sizes but not at the largest ones.
\end{keypoint}

\subsection{Halo Exchange}

A repeated neighbor exchange offers a different opportunity from ping-pong. The fixed submission and completion cost can be spread across many exchanges, while multiple peer transfers can proceed together. Figure~\ref{fig:m-halo-regimes} contrasts isolated and steady-state execution on one and eight nodes.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/results/halo-regimes.png}
\caption{Isolated and steady-state halo latency for all six measured stacks. Steady-state values amortize a batch of 100 exchanges. Points and shading show allocation medians and ranges; payload counts two sends and two receives.}
\label{fig:m-halo-regimes}
\end{figure}

MPI supplies the low-startup reference for isolated exchanges. Batching makes OSHMPI competitive across small and intermediate payloads because its batch-level completion cost is amortized. At large multi-node payloads, persistent NVSHMEM sustains the highest rate among the direct paths in Table~\ref{tab:m-halo-bandwidth}. Its approximately 41\,GB/s aggregate rate remains similar from two to eight nodes, while MPI reaches approximately 34--36\,GB/s.

The favorable regime for the persistent, kernel-invoked NVSHMEM path is thus sustained neighbor work rather than universally low latency. The SYCL MPI and oneCCL/NCCL curves further show that portable submission paths do not remove the same isolated-versus-steady distinction. This supports persistent execution as a candidate explanation in RQ1, while the host-driven OSHMPI result shows that batching and completion policy also matter independently of invocation location. Because the paths differ in signaling, persistence, sampling, and progress, these measurements do not isolate a launch-overhead saving by themselves. The inter-node advantage is achieved under the proxy-assisted configuration of Section~\ref{sec:ibgda-check}.

\begin{keypoint}[Halo takeaway]
No single stack owns this pattern; the batching contract decides it. MPI is the reference for isolated exchanges, where submission and completion are exposed. OSHMPI becomes competitive once a batch amortizes its completion cost, but saturates at 24.4\,GB/s across every multi-node topology, the lowest sustained rate measured. NVSHMEM, in its persistent device-invoked form, sustains the highest multi-node rate, 40.6--41.1\,GB/s and flat in node count, against 34.4--36.5\,GB/s for MPI. NCCL and oneCCL/NCCL sit between the two and keep the same isolated-versus-batched split, and SYCL MPI again tracks CUDA MPI.
\end{keypoint}

\subsection{Allreduce}

For reductions, the dominant distinction is between startup-sensitive scalar work and bulk combination. Figure~\ref{fig:m-allreduce-backends} shows all seven measured paths at representative intra- and multi-node topologies. NCCL-based paths lead the large-message regime (Table~\ref{tab:m-allreduce-bandwidth}). Within a node, NVSHMEM matches NCCL closely (128.3 against 127.5\,GB/s on \texttt{1n4g}). This is expected rather than a coincidence: in the benchmark configuration NVSHMEM hands its collectives to NCCL, so both curves measure NCCL's allreduce, and neither says anything about NVSHMEM's own collective implementation.

The agreement does not survive scale in this campaign. From \texttt{4n4g} onward the NVSHMEM series separates from NCCL and falls behind every other path, to 14.3 against 31.8\,GB/s at \texttt{4n4g} and 11.9 against 29.5\,GB/s at \texttt{8n4g}, with a 16\,MiB latency of 2733\,$\mu$s against NCCL's 1104\,$\mu$s. Its allocation spread is also much wider than its neighbors'. Because dispatch configuration is exactly the variable that makes the intra-node curves agree, a separation that appears only across nodes is more consistent with a change in the selected multi-node path than with the collective algorithm itself, and the archived records do not carry the per-run dispatch setting needed to decide that. The multi-node NVSHMEM reduction figures are therefore reported as measured and are not used to rank collective implementations.

The complete backend view also exposes the cost of oneCCL/OSHMPI's symmetric staging. On \texttt{1n4g}, the four-byte allreduce medians are 20.2\,$\mu$s directly and 18.6\,$\mu$s through oneCCL/OSHMPI. At 16\,MiB, they are 364\,$\mu$s and 6538\,$\mu$s, respectively: the adapted path is approximately 18 times slower. The direct device-resident reduction and symmetric-staging adapter use different memory and completion paths. The comparison therefore identifies the cost of the evaluated integration choice, not an intrinsic penalty of the oneCCL API.

This pattern demonstrates why functional interface compatibility and performance retention must be evaluated separately. The OSHMPI adapter waits for queue dependencies, stages ordinary operands through symmetric storage, performs a blocking host operation, and returns an already-complete event. Those semantics preserve correctness but remove the asynchronous opportunity retained by the NCCL path.

\begin{keypoint}[Allreduce takeaway]
The payload size splits this pattern in two. At four bytes, MPI and OSHMPI are fastest across nodes, at 12.4 and 12.6\,$\mu$s on \texttt{2n1g} against about 20\,$\mu$s for the others. At 16\,MiB the order reverses: NCCL leads with 1104\,$\mu$s at \texttt{8n4g} against 1879\,$\mu$s for MPI, and oneCCL/NCCL follows it closely. NVSHMEM matches NCCL within a node only because it dispatches to it, and separates from every path across nodes. oneCCL/OSHMPI is the one clear outlier, about 18 times slower than direct OSHMPI at 16\,MiB because of symmetric staging. For the MPI stacks the configuration matters more than the library: UCC changes the 16\,MiB result by up to 96.5 times.
\end{keypoint}

\subsubsection{UCC Impact}

The UCC control explains why MPI's result cannot be summarized by a single library label, and it is the largest configuration effect measured anywhere in this campaign. Figure~\ref{fig:m-ucc-allreduce} shows both configurations in absolute terms, because the ratio alone understates what is at stake. At a 16\,MiB intra-node reduction on \texttt{1n4g}, the same MPI installation takes 34.9\,ms with UCC disabled and 361\,$\mu$s with it enabled, a factor of 96.5. The corresponding multi-node figures are 39.6\,ms against 1121\,$\mu$s at \texttt{2n4g} and 45.2\,ms against 1873\,$\mu$s at \texttt{8n4g}. The non-UCC curve does not merely scale worse: it breaks down above 1\,MiB, where doubling the payload to 2\,MiB multiplies its cost by 5.1, from 809\,$\mu$s to 4101\,$\mu$s, and the gap widens monotonically from there.

The trade-off runs the other way at small payloads, which is why the policy is a choice rather than a default to enable everywhere. Around 4\,KiB, UCC is slower on every measured topology, by factors of 0.55, 0.54, and 0.46 at \texttt{1n4g}, \texttt{2n4g}, and \texttt{8n4g}. Intra-node the curve recovers quickly; on the two multi-node topologies UCC remains behind until roughly 64\,KiB. The reductions that \texttt{cg\_step} and aCG actually perform are single doubles, which sit firmly inside that penalty region, and this is why Section~\ref{sec:runtime-method} disables UCC for the composite step while enabling it for the standalone sweep.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/results/ucc-ab-allreduce.png}
\caption{Paired UCC \texttt{allreduce} control for CUDA MPI. Top: the measured cost of each configuration, so the divergence at large payloads is visible in absolute terms. Bottom: the resulting speedup, shaded green where UCC is faster and red where it is slower. Values are matched summary means; the SYCL MPI series tracks these curves closely and is omitted for legibility.}
\label{fig:m-ucc-allreduce}
\end{figure}

The discontinuity near 4\,KiB is consistent with the UCC TL/UCP source's change from a latency-oriented recursive k-nomial schedule to scatter-reduce/allgather k-nomial \cite{openucx_ucc_source_2026}. Source defaults explain a plausible mechanism, while forcing alternatives or recording the exact selected schedule would strengthen attribution for individual cells. The experiment uses one fixed UCC policy for the standalone allreduce sweep, so it does not claim a pointwise optimal configuration.

The single-rank \texttt{allreduce} control is anomalously slow for large buffers: the auxiliary UCC data report approximately 21\,ms at 16\,MiB, despite no inter-rank reduction. That cost is essentially unchanged by the UCC setting, at 21.9\,ms disabled against 21.4\,ms enabled, so it is not a collective-component effect. It is the same order of magnitude as the 34.9\,ms non-UCC four-rank result above, which is consistent with both being dominated by a local copy or reduction implementation rather than by transfer, though a one-rank control cannot separate those two by itself. It is excluded as a reference for network startup.

\subsection{All-to-All}

\texttt{alltoall} gives the clearest example of a configuration changing the apparent library ranking, and its UCC profile is the opposite of \texttt{allreduce}'s. Figure~\ref{fig:m-alltoall-backends} first shows all seven measured stacks against bytes per peer, rather than the topology-dependent aggregate send-buffer size. The MPI default changes regime sharply as rank count and per-peer size increase, while oneCCL/OSHMPI's staged path remains substantially more expensive.

At the largest \texttt{8n4g} payload, direct NCCL and oneCCL/NCCL are within approximately 2\%. The adapter and SYCL orchestration are therefore much less visible at this bulk endpoint than in four-byte ping-pong. This is again a complete-stack comparison, not an isolated adapter measurement. \begin{keypoint}[All-to-all takeaway]
NCCL and oneCCL/NCCL own the bulk regime, at 10.0 and 10.2\,GB/s on \texttt{8n4g} against 4.2\,GB/s for CUDA MPI, and are within 2\% of each other at the largest payload. NVSHMEM keeps pace within two nodes but falls to 5.7\,GB/s at 32 GPUs. MPI is only usable at small payloads once its algorithm selection is corrected, which is a configuration result rather than a library one. OSHMPI is the slowest measured path at the bulk endpoint, and oneCCL/OSHMPI remains expensive throughout because every block is staged through symmetric memory.
\end{keypoint}

\subsubsection{Algorithm Selection and UCC}

Figure~\ref{fig:m-ucc-alltoall} isolates what makes the MPI default collapse at small payloads. At \texttt{8n4g} the non-UCC path costs between 671 and 974\,$\mu$s for every payload at or below 256 bytes per peer and then drops abruptly to 60\,$\mu$s at 512 bytes, a cliff rather than a curve; with UCC enabled the same small sizes cost between 24 and 31\,$\mu$s. The peak gain is 33.7 times at 128 bytes per peer. Here the benefit is confined to the small-message, high-rank corner: the two configurations agree to within 2\% by 8\,KiB per peer, and at 256\,KiB per peer UCC is instead slower, at 0.87 of the non-UCC time at \texttt{8n4g} and 0.56 at \texttt{2n4g}. \texttt{allreduce} and \texttt{alltoall} therefore want UCC enabled in opposite regions of the same size axis, which is why a single machine-wide setting cannot be correct for both.

Modified Bruck reduces communication rounds by forwarding data, but the Open MPI implementation also rotates buffers, constructs indexed datatypes, and copies noncontiguous blocks \cite{bruck_alltoall_1997,openmpi_alltoall_source_2026}. Those local operations can be expensive on GPU-resident buffers. The UCC dispatch trace identifies its UCP transport layer, whose CUDA default uses contiguous peer slices and nonblocking tagged communication \cite{venkata_ucc_2024,openucx_ucc_source_2026}. This explains the structural difference; it does not replace a profile of individual copy costs.

\subsection{Scale-Up and Scale-Out}\label{sec:micro-scaling}

Reading the four patterns across the topology axis rather than the size axis separates two different effects. \emph{Scaling up} adds GPUs inside a node and keeps every transfer on NVLink; \emph{scaling out} adds nodes and puts part of the traffic on InfiniBand. The two behave differently, and a stack can be good at one and not the other. The steady-state halo at a 16\,MiB aggregate payload makes the first effect explicit. Within one node the aggregate rate is 325\,GB/s for OSHMPI and 280\,GB/s for CUDA MPI at \texttt{1n4g}. Moving the same exchange to \texttt{2n1g}, which keeps two ranks but places them on separate nodes, drops every stack to 20--24\,GB/s, an order-of-magnitude change produced by the interconnect alone. The rate then stays essentially flat as nodes are added: NVSHMEM holds 41.1, 40.9, and 40.6\,GB/s at \texttt{2n4g}, \texttt{4n4g}, and \texttt{8n4g}, and CUDA MPI holds 33.9--36.5\,GB/s over the same range. Once the exchange is inter-node, its aggregate rate is limited by what one node can inject, not by the number of nodes participating.

\texttt{allreduce} shows the second effect, and it runs in the opposite direction. At 16\,MiB, \texttt{2n1g} costs 1557\,$\mu$s for NCCL even though only two GPUs participate, whereas \texttt{8n4g} with 32 GPUs costs 1104\,$\mu$s. Adding intra-node ranks does not simply add work: the NVLink stage of a hierarchical reduction shrinks the payload that must cross InfiniBand. The same mechanism explains why the NCCL-based paths pull away from MPI only once each node holds four GPUs, from 567\,$\mu$s against 1118\,$\mu$s at \texttt{2n4g} to 1104\,$\mu$s against 1879\,$\mu$s at \texttt{8n4g}. In the scalar regime, where no such staging is available, the four-byte allreduce instead grows monotonically with rank count, from roughly 18--20\,$\mu$s at \texttt{1n4g} to 46--49\,$\mu$s at \texttt{8n4g} for every path except NVSHMEM, whose 88\,$\mu$s belongs with its separated multi-node behavior.

These two effects bound what the later levels can show. A stack that wins intra-node has not been shown to win at scale, because the NVLink regime it won in disappears at \texttt{2n1g}; and a stack that loses at two ranks may recover at 32, because hierarchical staging only becomes available when nodes hold several GPUs. The remaining topology panels are in Appendix~\ref{app:complete-results}.

For the five patterns that time communication alone, SYCL costs about 1\% at the median and stays within 8\% in all but one case. \texttt{pingpong} is the exception, where the largest messages reach $1.84\times$; small messages there sit in the MPI startup regime, which both stacks share, while the large-message endpoints diverge. On this evidence, expressing a GPU-aware MPI exchange through SYCL rather than CUDA is close to free, which is the expected result: the payload path, transport, and completion boundary are identical, and only the host-side submission and the buffer's allocator change.

The composite step is the one pattern with a clear gap, at $1.28\times$ median, and it is also the only pattern that includes computation. Its single-GPU control, where no inter-rank transfer occurs at all, separates the two contributions: at side 512 the step costs 116.9\,$\mu$s under SYCL MPI against 79.0\,$\mu$s under CUDA MPI, a $1.48\times$ ratio with communication removed entirely. The portability cost measured here therefore belongs to kernels and host orchestration, not to the communication path, and the same conclusion constrains the solver-level CUDA--SYCL ratios of Section~\ref{sec:acg-results}.

The oneCCL/NCCL column behaves differently, because it adds an adapter to the compute-interface change. It is within 2\% of direct NCCL for the three collective patterns, but reaches $1.32\times$ for the isolated halo and $1.10\times$ for ping-pong, the two cases where submission cost is exposed rather than amortized. A common interface is therefore inexpensive where the operation is large enough to hide its submission, and visible where it is not.

These primitive results leave several plausible candidates: MPI for startup-sensitive transfers, persistent NVSHMEM for large repeated halos, and NCCL-based paths for bulk collectives. The next question is whether composing operations preserves those advantages.

\section{MoE Dispatch and Combine}\label{sec:moe-results}

The two application microbenchmarks address different threats to the primitive rankings. This section covers \texttt{moe}, which keeps the redistribution operation but abandons the assumption that every peer receives the same number of bytes. Section~\ref{sec:cg-step-results} then covers \texttt{cg\_step}, the level that Section~\ref{sec:acg-results} pairs with the solver.

The \texttt{moe} archive is complete for the six declared stacks across all seven topologies and all three routing cases, with five allocations of three trials per cell. Total useful volume is held fixed at 32\,MiB per rank for dispatch and combine together, so a difference between routing cases is a difference in distribution, not in bytes moved.

\subsection{Backend Ranking}

Figure~\ref{fig:m-moe-sensitivity} is the central \texttt{moe} result. No stack is faster than CUDA MPI in all three routing cases, and three of the five compared stacks cross from below one to above one as the routing changes. The two NCCL-based paths are fastest under \texttt{uniform} routing, at almost exactly half of CUDA MPI's time, but under \texttt{locality80} direct NCCL is 1.55 times slower than CUDA MPI: when 80\% of tokens stay local, the remaining exchange is small and irregular, and NCCL's grouped point-to-point path no longer amortizes its submission cost over a bulk transfer. NVSHMEM shows the opposite profile, moving from 0.83 under \texttt{uniform} to 0.72 under \texttt{locality80}, the lowest measured value at \texttt{8n4g}, where per-destination puts suit many small, uneven blocks.

\begin{figure}[htbp]

The \texttt{hotspot80} case separates the stacks by a different mechanism. Directing 80\% of tokens at rank 0 makes every path substantially slower in absolute terms, to 34.0\,ms for CUDA MPI at \texttt{8n4g}, but it penalizes the two one-sided paths roughly twice as much: NVSHMEM reaches 62.0\,ms and OSHMPI 62.7\,ms, about 1.8 times CUDA MPI. The \texttt{alltoallv} implementations schedule a skewed exchange, whereas the SHMEM paths issue per-destination puts in the order the ranks produce them and have no collective algorithm available to reorganize the resulting incast. This is consistent with the structural difference, though the measurement does not separate scheduling from NIC-level congestion at the target.

OSHMPI is the one stack that is slower than CUDA MPI in every routing case at \texttt{8n4g}, by 1.70 to 1.84 times, despite matching MPI within one node at \texttt{1n4g} under two of the three cases. Its per-phase quiet, device synchronization, and barrier are inexpensive when every put lands on NVLink and expensive once each of them must complete across the fabric.

Two qualifications bound these results. \texttt{moe} keeps UCC disabled and uses the variable-count Open MPI path, so the MPI series is one fixed configuration rather than a tuned result, and the all-to-all tuning of Section~\ref{sec:micro-results} does not transfer to it untested. Second, the reported volume is useful application bytes including tokens that never leave their source rank, so the \texttt{locality80} latencies correspond to much less traffic than their byte count suggests; they are comparable across stacks, not convertible into a link rate.

\subsection{Comparison with All-to-All}

The standalone \texttt{alltoall} sweep of Section~\ref{sec:micro-results} moves equal blocks between all peers, and on that evidence the NCCL-based paths own the bulk regime. \texttt{moe} retains the same operation family and the same topologies, changes only the distribution of block sizes, and inverts that conclusion in two of three cases. The regime boundary that matters for this workload is therefore not the payload size that the primitive sweep varies, but the skew that it holds constant.

This is the same failure mode that Section~\ref{sec:cg-step-results} finds for \texttt{cg\_step}, reached from the opposite direction: there, matching the operation and payload is insufficient because submission structure changes; here, matching the operation and the total volume is insufficient because the distribution changes. Neither result is evidence against the primitive measurements. Both constrain what a primitive measurement can be asked to predict.

\begin{keypoint}[MoE takeaway]
No stack wins all three routing cases, so this pattern has no single answer. NCCL and oneCCL/NCCL are fastest under uniform routing, at about half of CUDA MPI, but direct NCCL becomes 1.55 times slower than MPI once most tokens stay local. NVSHMEM is the opposite, and gives the fastest multi-node result of all under locality-biased routing, at 0.72 of MPI. Under a hotspot, the two put-based paths, NVSHMEM and OSHMPI, are penalized about 1.8 times relative to the \texttt{alltoallv} implementations. OSHMPI is slower than MPI in every case at 32 GPUs, by 1.70 to 1.84 times, despite matching it within a node. MPI and SYCL MPI are never fastest but never collapse.
\end{keypoint}

\subsection{Device-Initiated Step}\label{sec:cg-device}

The device-initiated implementation of \texttt{cg\_step} issues the halo exchange and both scalar reductions from inside the kernel, so it is the benchmark-level counterpart of the monolithic device solver. It was measured over every topology and seven grid sides from 256 to 16\,384, with three allocations of three trials per cell, except at \texttt{8n4g}, where the smallest and largest sides are missing. It reports no phase timers, so it appears in step time only and not in the phase breakdown below.

Its result depends on both axes at once. Against its own host counterpart it is consistently better once communication crosses the fabric: at \texttt{8n4g} it takes 0.63 of host NVSHMEM's step time at side 512 and 0.52 at side 8192. Against the best host stack of each cell, however, it leads only where the problem is small and the GPU count low, at 0.67 of the fastest host time on \texttt{1n1g} and 0.82 on \texttt{1n4g} at side 512. It falls behind from eight GPUs upward at that side, at 1.12 to 1.25, and behind everywhere at side 8192, where its single-GPU step costs 1.95 times the fastest host stack. Device initiation therefore helps this step where per-GPU work is small enough for launch and synchronization costs to dominate, and does not compensate for a slower local execution once the stencil and dot products grow.

Figure~\ref{fig:m-cg-device-step} shows both regimes directly. At side 512 the device implementation starts as the fastest path by a wide margin on one GPU and then rises fastest of all, crossing the MPI and OSHMPI curves at eight GPUs: with only 512 columns to divide, adding GPUs removes local work but adds a halo exchange and two reductions that the kernel must complete before the next step. At side 8192 the ordering is reversed and stable: every host implementation is faster at every GPU count, but the device curve descends in parallel with them, which is the strong-scaling behavior the next comparison uses.

\subsection{Comparison with Microbenchmarks}\label{sec:cg-comparison-micro}

Matching operation size is insufficient when submission structure changes. The benchmark's MPI halo uses two serialized \texttt{MPI\_Sendrecv} exchanges in \texttt{cg\_step}, whereas the regular halo test groups its neighbor work differently. The auxiliary comparison reports a roughly 1.9-fold discrepancy, consistent with this operation-count difference. Similarly, equal eight-byte payloads do not by themselves match two float32 values with one float64 value. The cross-level comparison below therefore uses the composite step's one-double reductions.

CUDA--SYCL differences remain visible in the single-GPU controls, where inter-rank communication is absent. Earlier redundant queue waits were removed before the retained composite sweep, but the remaining gap cannot be assigned entirely to submission overhead or kernel arithmetic from two endpoint timings. The single-GPU result establishes a complete-implementation cost that must be considered alongside communication.

\begin{keypoint}[Composite-step takeaway]
OSHMPI has the lowest estimate in 25 of the 30 multi-rank cells, including every multi-node one, but under a contract the solver does not share: its reduction result stays in host memory. NCCL leads the remaining five intra-node cells and is the strongest path whose contract is complete. NVSHMEM is slower here and shows the widest allocation spread, so its ordering against the others is not decisive. MPI is the reference the other stacks are measured against, and its halo uses two serialized \texttt{MPI\_Sendrecv} calls rather than the grouped exchange of the halo benchmark. The SYCL stacks carry a measurable cost even at one GPU, where no inter-rank communication occurs.
\end{keypoint}

\subsection{Halo Structure and Overlap}

The regular halo and composite step do not reproduce aCG's exchange. The synthetic step has at most two fixed neighbors and a small structured payload. Table~\ref{tab:m-acg-halo-signatures} reconstructs the application signatures from the stored communication matrices, with element-to-byte conversion checked against the per-rank log totals. Bump reaches a mean of 10.8 outgoing neighbors at 32 GPUs, with as many as 17 on an individual rank. These asymmetric graphs are available as replay inputs in the reconciled archive. Nonblocking MPI and NCCL paths may progress while local SpMV executes, whereas the measured OSHMPI path completes before that work begins.

The nominal halo timer cannot resolve this difference by itself. It measures only the wait at halo-end. For OSHMPI, communication has already completed in halo-begin, so a small halo column coexists with a substantial cost in \emph{other}. Dividing the full payload by that residual wait produces an apparent bandwidth, not a physical transfer rate. A comparison of isolated halo latency with this column would therefore compare different intervals.

Auxiliary attempts to replace global barriers with neighbor flags or to split begin/end without deferring completion did not improve total solver time. They show why a change in timer placement is insufficient to create overlap. A useful next comparison must retain the same peer graph and measure submission, progress, completion, and exposed wait around independent local work.

These differences explain why the composite winner need not be the solver winner. The benchmark's scalar completion contract and the application's reduction primitive differ, while halo structure and overlap also change. The current evidence cannot assign the ranking reversal to any one of these factors.

\subsection{Convergence and Variability}

All 507 retained completed logs meet the recorded relative residual criterion, including 90 OSHMPI trials. This validates the recorded numerical outcome of those runs; it does not imply that every attempted run completed or that floating-point reduction order leaves iteration counts unchanged. Both total time and executed work are retained so that convergence differences remain visible.

MPI shows a separate performance-variability problem. Its halo and subsequent allreduce intervals grow together in slow runs, whereas the OSHMPI-halo solver uses the same MPI scalar interface with much more stable reduction timings. The contrast implicates the surrounding exchange and rank-arrival behavior, but does not isolate a particular UCX state or a single progress mechanism.

Large variations also occur among trials within one allocation, as Figure~\ref{fig:m-acg-scaling} shows. Node selection alone is therefore insufficient to explain the effect. Valid solutions can coexist with timing decompositions whose overlapping event regions are unsuitable for phase attribution. The chapter consequently reports MPI allocation ranges and avoids presenting its rare fastest run as a representative scaling curve. The irregular-halo replay is intended to test whether the behavior can be reproduced outside the complete solver.
