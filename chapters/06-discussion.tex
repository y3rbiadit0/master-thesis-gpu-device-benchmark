\chapter{Discussion}\label{ch:m-discussion}

The measurements in Chapter~\ref{ch:m-results} answer two connected questions: when does GPU-initiated communication help, and when can a benchmark guide application-level stack selection? This chapter follows the evaluation from individual patterns through \texttt{moe} and \texttt{cg\_step} to the aCG solver. At each step, it asks which rankings persist and what changes when the workload adds irregular exchanges, computation, or synchronization. It then brings together the GPU-initiated scaling and CUDA--SYCL comparisons, answers the research questions, and explains the limits of the conclusions.

\section{From Benchmarks to the Solver}

A library name alone does not predict performance; the algorithm, transport, memory placement, and synchronization behind it matter as well. Table~\ref{tab:m-benchmark-to-solver} retraces the evaluation from isolated operations to the solver and notes what each step adds. The fastest stack changed at every step, so a developer who wants to use a benchmark to choose a stack must first check how closely that benchmark resembles the application.

The individual patterns identify promising stacks for particular workloads: MPI for latency-sensitive ping-pong and isolated halos, persistent NVSHMEM for large batches of regular neighbor exchanges, and NCCL-based stacks for bulk collectives. Tuning MPI/UCC also avoids a severe small-message all-to-all slowdown. The application microbenchmarks and aCG then test whether those advantages persist as the workload changes.

Topology and payload distribution explain two important changes. Crossing from one node to several changes both bandwidth and the algorithms a library can use. The steady halo rate falls by roughly an order of magnitude at the first inter-node topology, then stays flat as nodes are added. Bulk allreduce can instead improve as nodes gain GPUs because hierarchical staging reduces the inter-node payload.

The MoE benchmark changes the payload distribution while holding operation, topology, and total volume fixed. The bulk ranking reverses in two of three routing cases. At \texttt{8n4g}, NCCL is fastest under uniform routing but 1.55 times slower than CUDA MPI under locality-biased routing. A hotspot distribution penalizes the two one-sided implementations by about 1.8 times.

The \texttt{cg\_step} benchmark combines the solver's halo exchange, local computation, and scalar reductions, but omits vector updates and convergence checks. Its MPI halo serializes two \texttt{MPI\_Sendrecv} calls, later computation does not use its reduced scalars, and the measured OSHMPI implementation leaves those results in host memory. The aCG solver adds irregular peers, rank-dependent message volumes, memory-bound local work, convergence, and opportunities to overlap communication with computation. OSHMPI's lowest point estimate in the \texttt{cg\_step} benchmark consequently does not predict the lower representative NCCL solver times: the two tests differ in reduction operation, result placement, peer structure, and synchronization.

Two comparisons do show agreement between benchmark and solver. The MPI and NCCL one-double reduction timings differ by 7.8--11.1\% on average over the five measured rank counts when operation, datatype, and timing endpoints match (Section~\ref{sec:reduction-agreement}). The device-initiated step reproduces the monolithic solver's strong-scaling shape to within 8.0\% and 12.6\% mean absolute deviation at grid side 8192, but differs by 582\% and 853\% at side 512 (Section~\ref{sec:cg-device-correlation}). In the second comparison, the benchmark, backend, and operation sequence stay the same; the amount of local work per GPU changes. This shows why matching communication alone is insufficient: the balance between computation and data transfer also matters. Both comparisons describe agreement in the measured cases, not predictions tested on new data. Using a benchmark to guide application selection requires checking its operations, datatypes, peers, progress mechanism, completion requirements, overlap, and local work per GPU against the application.

\begin{resultbox}[Main experimental result]
Communication benchmarks can identify promising stacks, but a faster isolated operation does not always make the application faster. The benefit depends on how much time the application spends waiting for that operation and whether its result is ready where the next computation needs it.
\end{resultbox}

\section{Device-Initiated Scaling}\label{sec:device-initiated-scaling}

Because the research questions ask when GPU-initiated communication pays off, and NVSHMEM is the only evaluated stack that offers it, its behavior is worth reading across the patterns rather than one at a time. Table~\ref{tab:m-nvshmem-scale} places NVSHMEM against the fastest measured stack at one node and at eight, for every pattern.

The column pair shows one consistent movement: with the halo as the single exception, every pattern places NVSHMEM further behind the leader when nodes are added than when GPUs are added within a node. The effect is largest where the measurement is latency-bound, from $2.6\times$ to $5.6\times$ in ping-pong, and smallest where it is bandwidth-bound, from $1.4\times$ to $1.8\times$ in all-to-all. Its intra-node bulk allreduce is the fastest path measured, and it loses that position entirely across nodes. The scalar case moves the same way: the four-byte allreduce costs 19.9\,$\mu$s on one node, in line with every other stack, and 88.2\,$\mu$s at 32 GPUs, where the others stay between 46 and 49\,$\mu$s.

The steady-state halo shows where NVSHMEM performs best. It runs batches of neighbor exchanges inside a persistent kernel, using signals to coordinate with peers. It is the only pattern NVSHMEM leads at eight nodes, at 40.6\,GB/s against 35.7\,GB/s for MPI, with throughput staying flat as nodes are added. The advantage is specific to sustained, batched neighbor traffic. In the solver, the monolithic device-NVSHMEM variant gains on blocking OSHMPI as process count grows, but does not overtake NCCL.

Three implementation choices may explain these results, and the campaign does not separate their effects. First, the CPU proxy documented in Section~\ref{sec:ibgda-check} services each inter-node request. Its overhead may matter most for small, frequent requests, where the measured gap is largest. Second, the benchmark enables NVSHMEM's dispatch of collectives to NCCL, so interpreting collective timings requires knowing which implementation actually ran. Third, the device-invoked variants change batching, signaling, and kernel organization along with invocation. The matched experiments in Section~\ref{sec:priority-experiments} would separate these contributions. The current measurements support a narrower conclusion: persistent NVSHMEM is effective for repeated neighbor exchanges, but loses ground in most other patterns when communication crosses nodes.

The scale-out results must be read in the context of Leonardo's transport support. IBGDA is unavailable in the evaluated environment, so inter-node device-issued requests use a CPU proxy (Section~\ref{sec:ibgda-check}), as previously reported for Leonardo \cite{trotter_cpu-_2025}. The larger gaps for small messages are consistent with overhead from servicing each request, although these measurements cannot isolate that cause. Published CG results on this class of system also identify missing device-side computational kernels and suboptimal NVSHMEM configuration as limits on their GPU-initiated variant \cite{trotter_cpu-_2025}. Direct GPU posting could reduce the proxy overhead, especially for frequent small transfers, but its benefit needs to be measured with the communication schedule held fixed. Repeating the sweep on systems with verified GPU-posted networking is the first follow-up proposed in Section~\ref{sec:priority-experiments}.

Within a node, device-issued transfers access mapped peer memory over NVLink without the network proxy. NVSHMEM is closer to the leaders here: its configured bulk allreduce, which dispatches to NCCL, is the fastest measured result, and its other intra-node gaps range from $1.4\times$ to $2.6\times$. Recent work also demonstrates the potential of device-side communication within a single NVLink domain: low-latency collectives built on NCCL's device API come within approximately 7\% of an estimated hardware lower bound \cite{shen_every_2026}. The exploratory NCCL measurements in Appendix~\ref{app:nccl-preliminary} show lower small-allreduce times with symmetric memory, but do not identify whether device invocation produced them. Together, these observations motivate separate evaluations of local and network communication, with particular attention to repeated neighbor traffic and latency-sensitive collectives.

\section{SYCL Portability Cost}\label{sec:sycl-cost}

The CUDA--SYCL comparison asks a different question: how much performance changes when the same communication is expressed through a portable programming interface. Over the five patterns that time communication alone, SYCL MPI costs about 1\% more than CUDA MPI at the median (Section~\ref{sec:sycl-portability}). The data transfers, transport, and completion requirements are the same; host submission and buffer allocation change. The \texttt{cg\_step} benchmark has a larger gap, at $1.34\times$ median over its multi-rank cells, and is also the first case that includes computation. This points to its kernels and host coordination as possible sources of the additional cost.

The solver comparison shows that the larger gap in the \texttt{cg\_step} benchmark is not an unavoidable cost of using SYCL (Section~\ref{sec:acg-sycl}). The SYCL solver runs close to native CUDA times within one node. It keeps the native data layout, including 32-bit sparse indices, and stores CG scalars in device memory. Both solvers require one host wait per iteration for the convergence test. Their close timings show the importance of implementation choices; the experiment does not isolate which choices account for the larger gap in \texttt{cg\_step}.

Across nodes, SYCL MPI keeps strong-scaling to 32 GPUs without the unusually slow runs observed in native MPI. The shared MPI installation and settings point to an interaction with the native solver's use of communication, although the cause remains unresolved.

The reduction implementation also changes the cost within the SYCL solver. With identical kernels and halo exchange, oneCCL/NCCL spends 85--340\,$\mu$s more per iteration blocked in the two reductions than MPI. Waiting for each oneCCL operation on the host introduces synchronization that native NCCL avoids through stream ordering. Changing the adapter to preserve that ordering would test how much solver time these waits add. The earlier native campaign also has faster NCCL times than the paired campaign, so cross-campaign ratios include variation beyond this measured adapter cost.

\section{Research Questions Revisited}

\paragraph{RQ1: When GPU initiation pays off.}
Persistent, kernel-invoked NVSHMEM performs well for large batches of regular halo exchanges, while MPI retains the advantage for small messages and isolated exchanges. In aCG, the monolithic device-NVSHMEM implementation improves relative to blocking OSHMPI as more GPUs are used but remains behind NCCL in the representative curves. The MoE hotspot case shows another limitation: when 80\% of tokens target one rank, the NVSHMEM and OSHMPI put-based implementations are about 1.8 times slower than the \texttt{alltoallv} implementations at 32 GPUs. Neither put-based implementation uses a collective algorithm to reorganize this concentrated traffic. The measured GPU-initiated benefits include changes in persistence, batching, signaling, and solver organization; isolating invocation and testing GPU-posted networking require the follow-up experiments in Section~\ref{sec:priority-experiments}.

\paragraph{RQ2: Practical trade-offs of SHMEM-style communication.}
SHMEM-style communication requires the application or an adapter to manage symmetric allocation, remote addresses, notifications, and safe buffer reuse. These responsibilities fit persistent or explicitly coordinated exchanges, but adapters can add substantial work. oneCCL/NCCL preserves asynchronous execution and approaches direct NCCL at the bulk all-to-all endpoint. In contrast, oneCCL/OSHMPI is approximately 18 times slower than direct OSHMPI for the measured 16\,MiB intra-node allreduce. The same common interface stays within 2\% of direct NCCL on the collective patterns. The integration choices therefore matter: copying data into symmetric buffers and blocking the host can outweigh the underlying library's direct performance.

\paragraph{RQ3: From benchmarks to applications, and SYCL performance retention.}
The benchmark and solver agree approximately for matched MPI and NCCL scalar reductions. The device-initiated step also reproduces the monolithic solver's scaling shape when their local work per GPU is comparable. The regular halo and synthetic OSHMPI result fail to predict the solver ranking because the peers, overlap, reduction operation, and completion requirements differ. Benchmarks can guide selection, but these examples show why their resemblance to the application must be checked.

For SYCL, the paired campaign reports solver-time ratios of 0.87--1.02 relative to native CUDA within one node, with every trial on both sides residual-valid. Across nodes, SYCL MPI avoids the slow native MPI runs. SYCL oneCCL/NCCL performs similarly to native NCCL in the paired allocations. Relative to the earlier native campaign, it reaches 46--84\% of native NCCL's performance at 8--32 GPUs, but that comparison also includes allocation differences. Section~\ref{sec:sycl-cost} distinguishes this variation from the additional host waits measured in oneCCL reductions.

\section{Limitations}\label{sec:limitations}

The results cover one system (Section~\ref{sec:eval-environment}), CPU-proxy progress for inter-node NVSHMEM (Section~\ref{sec:ibgda-check}), two sparse matrices, and redistribution tested only through microbenchmarks. Other systems may change both absolute times and stack rankings. The useful guidance is which workload and runtime features to check when making a selection.

Three further limits apply. First, the device-invoked NVSHMEM variants change persistence, batching, and signaling along with invocation. The measurements identify when these implementations perform well, but not how much improvement comes from each change. Second, agreement between benchmarks and solver is assessed over five GPU counts and two grid sides. These examples identify important matching conditions without validating a general prediction method. Third, each benchmark cell has five independent allocations, or three for \texttt{alltoall} and \texttt{cg\_step}, and no hypothesis tests are computed. Small timing differences cannot support firm rankings; the conclusions rely on large separations and clear changes with workload or topology. The exploratory NCCL measurements in Appendix~\ref{app:nccl-preliminary} are outside this evaluation.

These limitations motivate the five research directions in Section~\ref{sec:priority-experiments}: validation on IBGDA-enabled clusters, isolation of initiation effects, stronger benchmark--application correspondence, evaluation of newer device-communication interfaces, and a device-initiated path behind a portable collective interface.

\begin{keypoint}[Summary]
On Leonardo, the best communication stack depends on the workload and runtime settings. GPU initiation helps some repeated exchanges, but application-level gains also depend on computation, synchronization, and how much communication remains on the critical path.
\end{keypoint}
